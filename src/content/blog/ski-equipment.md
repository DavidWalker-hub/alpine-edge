## What is your ski doing for you?

Crammed into my ski locker in Val d’Isère, there are currently 8 pairs of skis of varying lengths, widths, and radii, so I am fully aware that I am not the typical consumer. I am, however, well placed to talk on what different skis can offer you. I don’t mean what brand to look for or who makes the coolest top sheet; I mean the fundamentals behind the design of the ski. So much of your enjoyment of a ski is subjective and based on several factors. How stiff is it? Where is the sweet spot? What is the flex pattern like? The only true fire way to know if you will like a particular ski is to give it a go, even with plenty of research and recommendations you can get it wrong. 8 pairs. That said, knowing the type of ski you are after can massively help reduce the chances of disappointment.

## What’s that underfoot?

When looking at a ski, typically there are four numbers that are relevant, aside from the price, and these will be printed, normally, on the tail of the ski. The radius, which we will discuss later, and three width measurements from different points on the ski, often written in the form XX-XX-XX. It is the middle set, the width of the ski under your foot, that define what the ski is for. A narrow measurement, around 70mm is a piste ski, a width of around 80-90mm would be classed as an all-mountain ski and anything over 95mm, you’re moving into full off-piste territory. While there is some nuance to this and some exceptions, think freestyle, this a reasonable set of guidelines.

## The one ski quiver

Now we know a bit about the width of a ski, it would be reasonable to assume, if we want to avoid the overflowing ski locker situation we should choose an all-mountain ski. This, in my mind, is a trap that many fall into. Don’t get me wrong, there are some excellent skis, I swear by my all-mountain Nordica enforcer 88, the 88 being that underfoot width, but all-mountain unfortunately does not mean all of the mountain all of the time. It is the perfect ski for those rare occasions a huge snowfall happened just after the groomers parked up for the night and the pistes have a foot of powder, they are great in mixed off-piste conditions, and they are acceptable on a groomed run. Would I enjoy skiing them down La face de Bellevarde the week after the world cup, on injected ice? No. No, I would not. I say all this to say that the idea of a one ski quiver is unfortunately a misnomer.

## Why a piste ski?

If you are joining us at Alpine Edge this winter, our retreat is based in Europe, specifically in Val d’isère, France. Obviously, it is impossible to know the forecast and snow conditions, yet we can say with confidence, there will be fantastic, groomed runs available during the week. This is far from the only reason we recommend a piste ski at Alpine Edge. As our skiing improves, the inputs become more subtle and precise. It is significantly easier to learn and improve these movements while skiing on a responsive piste ski rather than anything wider. It is also a lot easier to get those high angle Instagram shots and lets face it, that might just be the most important aspect in our social media driven world!

Before we finish our dive into the world of skis, I did mention radius, so I will explain it. Although I could bore you with technical diagrams about how it’s measured, the most important thing to understand is that the radius will dictate the type of turn a ski wants to do. A shorter radius, around 12m, will want to make tight turns, be very easy to put on a high edge angle but feel a bit unstable at speed. Conversely, a longer radius of say 20m, would need more patience building the edge angle, feel more comfortable with larger turns and be significantly more stable at speed. There is no right or wrong here, but it is likely you will favour one over another. For myself, the sweet spot is that 19-20m radius, while Alpine Edge coach David Hudson prefers a slalom ski of 13m.
